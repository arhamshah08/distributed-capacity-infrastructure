Distributed Capacity Infrastructure: Unlocking Grid Flexibility Through Open-Standards Dispatch

Discovery, valuation, and verification of distributed energy capacity in the data center era
Arham Shah
M.S. Energy Resources Engineering, Stanford University
November 2026

Abstract. Data center load growth, estimated at 50–100 GW in North America over the coming decade, stresses grid capacity at three nested layers at once: distribution hosting capacity, transmission tie limits, and generation adequacy. Distributed energy resources (DERs) already installed behind the meter could relieve all three, but operators cannot count them: an asset whose presence, availability, and response cannot be verified earns no hosting-capacity credit, no ELCC credit, and no market settlement.

The gap persists because each DER manufacturer exposes a proprietary cloud API, so every aggregator rebuilds the same integrations and no single party captures enough of the value of a common layer to build one. IEEE 2030.5 is the default utility-to-DER protocol under CPUC Rule 21, yet production gateways implement only one of its two roles, and OEM-cloud dispatch cannot meet the rate, custody, and audit requirements that FERC Order 2222 settlement imposes.

DC OPF with N-1 screening of the Bay Area subnetwork of the ACTIVSg10k case (703 buses, 12,564 MW peak load, 7,987 MW local generation) shows 36% of peak load imported across 42 tie branches, 3 of which bind at peak. An enrollment model of 360 synthetic households in MCE territory finds 242 kW of qualifying flexible capacity whose data confidence rises from 53% to 97% at IEEE 2030.5 enrollment, implying about 96 MW addressable across MCE's 134,131 households with installed devices. A bidirectional edge-gateway architecture specified against IEEE 2030.5, IEEE 1547-2018, OpenADR, and FERC Order 2222 closes the verification gap at the device.

At 100,000 enrolled assets (about 2.5 GW), direct displacement of marginal gas generation is 0.15–0.20 MtCO₂e/yr; curtailment recovery and infrastructure deferral raise the system effect to about 5×, or 0.75–1.0 MtCO₂e/yr. The rate-limiting step is the open dispatch layer, not hardware: more than 100 million DERs are already deployed worldwide.

Keywords: distributed energy resources; IEEE 2030.5; FERC Order 2222; hosting capacity; virtual power plants; data center load; resource adequacy

The Three-Layer Capacity Problem

Grid capacity is usually discussed as one bottleneck: interconnection queues, transmission congestion, or resource adequacy. It is three nested constraints, and relief at one layer moves the binding constraint to the next. Figure 1 shows the mechanism that ties them together.

Distribution Capacity

A feeder's hosting capacity is the DER export it can absorb before voltage or thermal limits bind. PG&E publishes it through Integration Capacity Analysis (ICA) at the circuit level [11], but as a static snapshot, while the true limit moves hour by hour with load and solar output. Midday export reverses flow through distribution transformers and raises secondary voltage at points the feeder head never sees. Static limits applied to a dynamic system deny connections that would be safe in most hours of the year, and for large loads seeking distribution-voltage service the same conservatism adds 12–24 months to interconnection.

Transmission Capacity

DC OPF with N-1 contingency screening on the Bay Area subnetwork of the ACTIVSg10k synthetic Western Interconnection case [1], solved in MATPOWER 8.0 [2], sets the scale of the transmission constraint (Figure 2). The subnetwork holds 703 buses and 12,564 MW of peak load against 7,987 MW of local generation (6,714 MW gas, 1,033 MW wind, 240 MW coal). The 4,577 MW difference, 36% of peak load, must be imported across 42 tie branches. Of 11,806 branch contingencies screened, 3 tie branches bind at peak. Headroom on those 3 branches during peak hours, not annual energy, sets how much new load the region can accept.

The result reframes data center siting. A planned 100 MW load split across 3–4 substations, with a firm commitment to curtail in the hours when those 3 ties bind, adds less peak tie loading than the same load connected rigidly, and can be studied against an effective hosting limit above the static ICA figure.

The market route for that commitment exists. FERC Order 2222 opened RTO and ISO energy, capacity, and ancillary markets to DER aggregations [3], and new supply cannot close the gap alone: nearly 2,600 GW of generation and storage sat in US interconnection queues at the end of 2023 [8]. CAISO curtailed 2.8 TWh of renewable output in 2023 [9], energy that flexible load at the right nodes and hours could have absorbed. Both routes fail at the same requirement. Dispatch must follow open standards and settlement must rest on audit-grade telemetry, while today each OEM exposes a proprietary cloud API that each aggregator integrates separately.

Generation Capacity

LOLP and ELCC measure whether supply covers load under uncertainty. Data center load growth degrades LOLP even with no generator retirements. Distributed storage is the fastest addition to reserve margin, and incentive programs such as California's SGIP have already paid to install it, but ELCC credit requires demonstrated availability. A battery that cannot be dispatched and verified on demand contributes zero firm capacity at any nameplate rating.

At all three layers the failure is identical: capacity that is physically installed cannot be counted. Counting it requires a valuation that states what each asset is worth, where it sits, and how certain the operator can be that it will respond.

A Framework for Distributed Capacity Valuation

A four-dimension score turns heterogeneous public and private data into one capacity signal per asset or site (Table 1). The dimensions are mutually exclusive and together cover what an aggregator or ISO must know before bidding a distributed resource. In use they are not independent: data confidence multiplies the other three, because value that cannot be verified cannot be bid.

Grid Value and Data Confidence in a Community VPP

MCE's VPP tariff pays $5–$20 per device per month for enrolled flexible assets, with a $40 cap ($50 for CARE/FERA income-qualified households) [10]. An enrollment model of 360 synthetic households, seeded to Census ACS distributions on real building footprints in seven MCE communities, finds 113 households (31%) qualifying on devices already installed, holding 242 kW of flexible capacity. Mean data confidence before enrollment is 53%; IEEE 2030.5 device discovery at enrollment raises it to 97% per device. Weighted by confidence, bankable capacity rises from 128 kW to 235 kW, a 1.8× gain from verification alone (Figure 3). Scaled to MCE's 134,131 households, the model implies about 96 MW of addressable flexible capacity.

The value function is superadditive. A household with a battery, EV charger, and smart thermostat performs more reliably in events under joint dispatch than the same devices dispatched separately. A flat per-device tariff misprices that reliability; a per-kW structure closer to FERC 2222 wholesale settlement pays for it and strengthens the incentive to enroll.

Site Scoring for Distributed Storage

At C&I and data center scale the same framework produces four outputs per candidate site: capacity_mw, deploy_weeks, cost_usd_kw, and revenue_usd_kw_yr. Revenue combines tariff savings, SGIP incentive annuity, CAISO capacity clearing prices, and demand response payouts, each contingent on verified delivery. Inputs are 21 datasets across five tracks: geospatial, community and policy, energy markets, cloud demand, and building models. Transmission headroom enters the revenue term directly, so a site next to a constrained substation with active ISO demand response programs scores higher revenue and shorter deployment.

The confidence gap is therefore a protocol gap. At 53% confidence, nearly half of the modeled 242 kW cannot be bid as firm capacity, because the operator cannot verify what is installed or whether it will respond, and no device discovery protocol works across manufacturers without per-vendor integration. Closing the gap requires a common protocol layer at the device itself.

Open-Standards Infrastructure as the Capacity Commons

Valuation states what capacity is worth; dispatch infrastructure determines whether it can be reached, verified, and traded. IEEE 2030.5 on an open edge gateway plays the role for DERs that TCP/IP played for networked computers: one protocol layer that makes any asset legible to any platform, independent of manufacturer.

Four instruments define the chain from device to market (Table 2). A gateway that carries capacity from a household to a wholesale settlement must satisfy all four at once.

The Bidirectionality Problem

The decisive architectural property is bidirectionality. Production gateways implement one IEEE 2030.5 role: either the client that answers a utility server or the server that manages client devices. A gateway that holds both roles (Figure 4) acts as a client to the utility or aggregator DERMS while serving as the 2030.5 server for devices on site. One firmware image then fits rooftop solar, utility-scale BESS, EV charging, and distribution transformer monitoring without per-site integration.

Southbound diversity is absorbed by a translation engine: SunSpec Modbus to inverters, CAN 2.0B to the battery management system for cell-level telemetry, OCPP to EV chargers, and DNP3 to legacy monitoring IEDs. A canonical DER model normalizes all of them, so no DR platform, DERMS, or ISO settlement system needs to know the manufacturer. Under FERC 2222 the ISO therefore receives telemetry in one format for every asset in an aggregation.

Why Edge Beats Cloud

The alternative is OEM-cloud dispatch, in which each manufacturer presents IEEE 2030.5 northbound from its cloud. It fails grid operations in four ways: rate limits make real-time dispatch unreliable; telemetry leaves the jurisdiction; the OEM can revoke API access unilaterally; and one compromised cloud reaches every asset on the platform. An edge gateway with a Trusted Execution Environment (TEE) removes all four (Figure 5). Each device holds its own cryptographic identity, so a compromise stays inside one site. Telemetry is processed on site and only normalized event data leaves. The asset operator owns the gateway outright, so no third party can revoke access.

Network Effects of Standardization

Once each DER speaks IEEE 2030.5 through an open gateway, any operator, aggregator, or DR platform can discover and transact with it without a bilateral integration agreement. Each enrolled asset enlarges the flexibility pool available to every participant, so value accrues to the shared layer rather than to one application. A commons makes flexibility tradeable, but trade needs a buyer that will pay for capacity at a specific node and hour, and until recently no such buyer existed at scale.

Data Center Flexibility as the Forcing Function

Data centers are that buyer. A large load waiting on interconnection values node- and hour-specific flexibility at transmission rates, which gives the open dispatch layer a revenue anchor that residential demand response never provided.

The Siting Problem as a Power Flow Problem

For a 100 MW load in the Bay Area subnetwork, the binding elements are the 3 tie branches identified in Section 1.2, and splitting the load across 3–4 substations lowers the peak contribution on any one of them. The node model has three terms: contracted capacity at each substation, unused capacity tradeable only within the node, and branch thermal limit as the hard constraint. A flexibility contract's value to the system operator follows from the loss-of-load expectation (LOLE) it removes over the operating year, so a commitment to curtail on named branches in named hours is worth more than generic demand response.

The contract is two-sided. The data center interconnects earlier because enrolled flexible capacity raises effective hosting capacity on the constrained feeder; the aggregator gains a firm counterparty for committed capacity. One gateway serves both sides: the device that executes the VPP dispatch also produces the record the ISO settles against (Figure 6).

Revenue Architecture Across Three Capacity Markets

One enrolled gateway reaches three markets (Table 3). For a 1 MW battery, ISO capacity and ancillary streams sum to $70–$230/kW-yr before energy margin (Figure 7); for a single household, the per-device distribution stream of $60–$240 per year is the only one accessible. Stacking is bounded by state of charge, since a kilowatt held for regulation cannot also be discharged for a capacity event, so realized revenue depends on co-optimized scheduling rather than simple addition.

The same dispatch that settles a flexibility contract also changes which generator runs at the margin, which is where the climate effect originates.

Climate Impact

The climate effect runs through three pathways that differ in timescale and size (Table 4, Figure 8). All three scale with the number of enrolled, verified assets.

Direct Dispatch Displacement

Dispatched flexibility displaces marginal generation, in WECC predominantly gas peakers at 0.4–0.7 tCO₂e/MWh. At 100,000 enrolled assets averaging 25 kW (about 2.5 GW), direct displacement is estimated at 0.15–0.20 MtCO₂e/yr. Back-calculated, that range implies 210–500 GWh of displaced peaker output per year, or 85–200 full-capacity hours, a conservative assumption relative to daily cycling.

Renewable Curtailment Reduction

CAISO curtailed 2.8 TWh of renewable output in 2023 [9]. Each TWh absorbed by flexible load instead of curtailed avoids about 0.5 MtCO₂e of gas generation, so full recovery would be worth 1.4 MtCO₂e/yr. The transmission headroom dimension of the capacity score identifies which sites sit at the constrained nodes where curtailment occurs, so deployment can be targeted there first.

Infrastructure Deferral

Hosting capacity measured from real-time distribution transformer telemetry lets utilities defer substation and transformer upgrades by 3–5 years per constrained feeder, valued at $30–$80 per kW of unlocked hosting capacity per year. Deferral avoids the embedded carbon of early equipment replacement and frees capital for renewable interconnection. Measured headroom also shortens the 12–24 month interconnection reviews that delay solar and storage projects.

The long-run figure follows from the per-asset system effect. At 100,000 assets the system level corresponds to 7.5–10 tCO₂e per asset per year. Applied to the more than 100 million DERs already deployed worldwide at half that intensity, to reflect smaller average asset size, the effect reaches 0.4–0.5 GtCO₂e/yr. The rate-limiting step is adoption of an open dispatch layer, not hardware: the assets exist, and the coordination infrastructure to activate them does not. Every pathway therefore runs through one step in the stack that has not yet been demonstrated at utility grade.

Evidence and Current Stage

Evidence from the Analyses

Three analyses support the argument, each addressing a different link. The power flow study establishes that the transmission constraint is concentrated: 3 of 42 ties bind at peak in a subnetwork importing 36% of its peak load, so flexibility is valuable at specific branches and hours rather than everywhere. The enrollment model establishes that verification, not device count, limits usable capacity: bankable capacity rises 1.8× at enrollment with no new devices, and about 96 MW is addressable in one CCA territory. The site-scoring model establishes that transmission headroom enters revenue directly, linking the physical constraint to the market signal.

Each result carries a stated limitation. ACTIVSg10k is a synthetic network, and DC OPF omits losses and voltage limits, so the binding set should be confirmed with AC power flow on utility planning cases. The household population is synthetic, seeded to ACS distributions, and the 53% and 97% confidence levels are model outputs that require validation against metered enrollment data. The climate estimates rest on marginal emission factors and dispatch-hour assumptions given in Section 5.

Why the Gap Persists

Three conditions keep the market from closing the gap on its own:

  1. Misaligned incentives. A proprietary cloud API is a retention asset for a device manufacturer, so no OEM gains from building a neutral layer that lets customers switch aggregators.
  2. Diffuse value. The benefit of a common layer is split among utilities, ISOs, aggregators, and customers, and no single party captures enough of it to fund the layer.
  3. Fixed certification burden. Utility-grade certification of both 2030.5 roles against a CPUC-approved DERMS is a fixed effort that single-role products have had no reason to undertake.

Open Research Questions

The architecture is specified and the analyses above are complete. Three questions remain open, and resolving the first removes the last technical barrier to FERC 2222 participation for sub-MW aggregations:

  1. Can the full bidirectional IEEE 2030.5 handshake, client and server roles in one device, be certified at utility grade against a CPUC-approved DERMS?
  2. How much effective hosting capacity does verified, dispatchable flexibility add on a constrained feeder under hourly AC power flow, relative to the static ICA limit?
  3. What ELCC does a verified aggregation of small DERs earn relative to its nameplate, and how does that credit change with data confidence?
